Interactive vs. static explanations

Does making model explanations interactive help people understand and trust them more? I built both versions and tested them with participants.

Type
M.Sc. thesis, 2026
Stack
Python, scikit-learn, SHAP, LIME, Plotly, Dash, Streamlit
Data
IBM HR Analytics Attrition (1,470 records, 35 features)
Read
Thesis PDF ↗

The question

Machine learning models are used in high-stakes decisions, but the most accurate ones tend to be the hardest to interpret. Explanation methods like SHAP and LIME help, but most research asks how to build better explanations. My thesis asked something different: does making an explanation interactive help people understand and trust it more?

What I built

  • Trained Logistic Regression and Random Forest classifiers on the IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition dataset (1,470 records, 35 features).
  • Used SHAP and LIME to produce global and local explanations of the predictions.
  • Built two parallel ways to see the same explanations. Static: fixed Matplotlib and Seaborn plots, such as feature-importance bars, SHAP summary plots, and correlation heatmaps. Interactive: a live dashboard (Plotly, Dash, Streamlit) with filtering, hover tooltips, and instance-level SHAP plots for what-if exploration.

How the user study worked

  • Participants: students and professionals with basic data literacy.
  • Design: every participant used both the static and the interactive version, with the task order counterbalanced to reduce learning effects.
  • Task: identify the main features driving a prediction and describe the model's behavior.
  • Measured: interpretation accuracy, completion time, and error rates, plus Likert questionnaires on usability, confidence, and trust. Open-ended answers were analyzed for themes.

What I found

MeasureStaticInteractive
Task interpretation accuracyLowerHigher
Task completion timeSlowerFaster
Reported trust and usabilityLowerHigher

The direction was consistent for most participants. In the open-ended answers, people said hovering, tooltips, dynamic filtering, and instance-level exploration made them more confident about why the model decided what it did. They called the static plots informative but limiting once they wanted to dig deeper. The full statistics are in the thesis.

A side finding about model choice

ModelAccuracyAttrition-class recall
Logistic Regression86%0.34
Random Forest83%0.09

The more complex Random Forest did worse on the minority class, which is the one that matters when you want to predict who will leave. On imbalanced data, accuracy alone can mislead.

Limits

  • The user study had a fairly small sample, so the results shouldn't be generalized to larger groups.
  • It used a single business dataset, so I can't say the findings hold in areas like healthcare or finance.
  • Class imbalance in the data affected how well the models predicted attrition.
  • I tested a limited set of models and explanation techniques.

The deployed prediction tool this work builds on is the Employee Attrition Dashboard.
